So, we're going to be banning social media in order to protect our children. Or, at least it seems that we might be heading in that direction after Australia introduced the world's first ban for under 16s at the end of last year. Some of our EU neighbours have gotten the ball rolling on similar legislation themselves - the National Assembly in France has adopted a bill to ban under 15s, and the Spanish govenment have announced intentions to pursue an under 16 ban. Many other other countries, including the UK, Denmark, and ourselves, are at various stages of going down the route of some form of a ban.

### Why the bans?

Why exactly are we banning social media for kids? The answer may seem obvious - it's dangerous of course! But how dangerous is it? To answer that question, we can look to a number of sources. First let's listen to what those who are leading the charge in introducing these bans have to say.

**Anthony Albanese**, Prime Minister of Australia wrote that social media is:

<Quote>
  a weapon for bullies, a platform for peer pressure, a driver of anxiety, a vehicle for scammers
  and, worst of all, a tool for online predators
</Quote>

French President **Emmanuel Macron** announced:

<Quote>
  The brains of our children and teenagers are not for sale. Their emotions are not for sale,
  neither to American platforms nor Chinese algorithms.
</Quote>

And, Prime Minister **Pedro Sánchez** of Spain said social media is a place of:

<Quote>...addiction, abuse, pornography, manipulation [and] violence.</Quote>

This is heavy stuff. You would be forgiven for thinking that it might be better to rip up the entire internet infrastructure, encase it in concrete, and shoot it out into space.

Behind the political rhetoric, there does lie a general idea that there are problems associated with social media use, and understanding the dangers and pitfalls requires a certain level of mental maturity. As Anthony Albanese noted,

<Quote>
  ...as we get older most of us get better at spotting the fakes and the risks and we build up the
  resilience to ignore the nastiness.
</Quote>

So, the dangers that exist can be more safely navigated by older teens. While this may be true for some of the dangers, this approach risks complacency which could lead to a different set of problems. I'll return to this later.

### What does the science say?

Is there any empirical evidence to back up the effectiveness of a ban on social media for children? Short answer, no. Since these large scale bans have only just come into force, the true impact will only become clear in years to come. There are some early reports of some users feeling much better now they're off their phones, while others are finding ways around the bans. However, it is wise to take these individual stories with a pinch of salt. We won't know the results for a few years.

The good news is we do have an abundance of scientific research into the effects of technology and social media use on teenagers. However, problematically, the results and conclusions often differ drastically. Take the following three academic articles (which have all been quoted in various media outlets) as an example. First, [a large meta-analysis of published results]('https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000468.pdf') carried out by an international team of researchers in 2025 found screen use leads to 'socioemotional problems'. This, in turn, leads to further screen use, and worse socioemotional problems. It's a viscious downward cycle. Second, [a 2025 University of Manchester study]('https://academic.oup.com/jpubhealth/advance-article/doi/10.1093/pubmed/fdaf150/8371934?login=false') on data from over 25,000 adolescents concluded the opposite, that there is no significant impact of technology use on mental health. Finally, [A 2026 large scale (over 100,000 participants), longitudinal study (3 years) of Australian adolescents]('https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2843720') at the University of South Australia found that moderate social media use was associated with the best well-being, better than both no use and excessive use. So, where does that leave us? Well, probably a bit confused.

Amidst the conflciting conclusions, one consistency does emerge across the majority of the scientific literature - the negative effects of _excessive_ use of internet, games, screens, social media etc. The term excessive use is employed when these activities begin to interfere with normal daily functioning. And, waht is the driver behind the addictive nature of social media?... The **'algorithms'**.

### Can't we just ban the algorithms?

If we are keen to stop excessive or problematic social media use, can't we just make the algorithms that feed us addictive content illegal instead of banning the whole platform? After all, a lot of social media is good - it enables us to stay in contact with distant friends and family, easily arrange events etc. It seems a shame to ban under 16s from these elements. One teenager, speaking on a podcast about the mobile phone bans in schools here in Ireland, told a story about her continued friendship with a girl who had left her school to move to another part of the country. Social media was the main reason they felt like they were still present in each others daily lives.

Banning the toxic algorithms and leaving the beneficial parts seems like a reasonable solution. TD Paul Murphy recently proposed such a measure in the Dail. He asked for the Taoiseach to support his party's bill:

<Quote>"to have toxic algorithms on social media platforms switched off."</Quote>

Unfortunately, in reality, this is not a feasible solution. In fact, from a technical standpoint, it is quite a naive proposition. We would effectively be banning math. The family of algorithms used to recommend content to users based on theirs and others' history of activity are called _'recommender algorithms'_. These are simply statistical models which perform probablistic calculations. They can also be very useful - recommendations for music, movies, and series on streaming platforms can be a good way to encounter new artists, directors, and actors. Same for online bookstores, clothes shops etc. Could we ban recommender algorithms for one platform but not another? It would also be very hard to determine what is and is not a 'toxic algorithm'. ChatGPT is a Large Language Model (LLM), but it can also act as a recommender system. (you can even ask it now to recommend 10 ways to protect your online privacy, and it will do a pretty decent job). So, would we ban LLMs? Additionally, this type of regulation would be impossible to police. Private tech companies keep their proprietary code under wraps. Unless we force companies to make all code open source, this proposal is a non-starter.

### What can be done?

Education. I will admit I am very biased on this topic. As an educator, when I watch the news or read articles on the need for social media bans, I am always on the lookout for information on what the education for children is going to be. It has been frustrating and disappointing to see a paucity of coverage and a lack of details on this matter to date. Australia, despite leading the way and already having a ban in place, has not published updates to its curriculum in relation to the age restrictions. The [eSafety Commisioner's wesite]('https://www.esafety.gov.au/educators/social-media-age-restrictions), which provides educational resources for schools, still shows a "(updates in relation to social media age restrictions coming soon)" notice.

Banning an activity for children prior to figuring out the details on how to prepare them for it is putting the cart before the horse. When we (the adults) restrict a potentially dangerous activity until a certain age, e.g. alcohol consumption, sexual relationships, we are obligated to provide an education so that children are informed and prepared when they do come of age. For alcohol, kids learn the details of what a 'unit' of alcohol is, how much is in each type of drink and the short and long term effects on the health of the body. With sex education, we teach the details of the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and STDs, alongside consent and relationships. These topics are taught for years before coming of age.

Children themselves have been requesting more, better, and earlier education on digital safety for a long time, including here in Ireland. The Ombudsman for Children's Office (OCO) said [blanket bans for phones in schools was not the best solution]('https://www.rte.ie/news/ireland/2025/0910/1532761-phone-bans-ireland/'). A report into this issue, '[
One Size Does Not Fit All (2025)]("https://www.oco.ie/app/uploads/2025/09/OCO-Smartphone-Ban-Child-Friendly-Report.pdf")' listed five recommendations by a Youth Advisory Panel which includes the following:

- _"Invest more in resources for digital education"_
- _"Increase support, education and awareness for parents, teachers and other adults to better understand technology"_

CyberSafeKids - Ireland's online safety charity - published a [Trends and Usage Report](https://www.cybersafekids.ie/report2025/) in 2025 where they state "Starting digital media and literacy education at secondary level is simply too late".

### Will updated school curriculums be effective?

It seems highly unlikely. The available resources for teachers and parents in Ireland, e.g. [Webwise]('https://www.webwise.ie'), [National Parents Council]('https://www.npc.ie/training-and-resources/training-we-offer/internet-safety-in-your-school') and [Comisiún na Meán ]('https://www.cnam.ie/general-public/children-young-people/keeping-safe-online/') provide some useful high-level information. They provide a lot of good materials for teachers, parents and students on topics like how to deal with cyberbullying and the types of content which are appropriate and inappropriate to share online. What is lacking is technical details. Using the earlier analogy of sex education, it would be like teaching consent and relationships, but not the details of the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and STDs. Sure, it is good to tell someone not to do something bad, but with a deeper understanding of the inner workings, the impact may be minimal.

To give a concrete example of why education on the technical details matter, we can stick to the topic in question - problematic social media use stemming from 'toxic algorithms'. These algorithms rely on knowing information about you in order to effectively keep you engaged. The more they know, the better the recommendations will be and the more you will keep scrolling. Companies need to know who you are, where you have been, and what you have been doing online in order to effectively target content at you. The amount of data associated to you is called your _'digital footprint'_

Minimising your footprint is very important. Let's say your child doesn't use Instagram, TikTok, Whatsapp etc until they are 16. When they do turn 16 and sign, they begin with a clean slate, right? And As long as they are sensible in the content they choose to watch, and be careful about what they themselves upload, they should be fine...? No. Not at all. Meta, Google, Tik Tok track users across the internet - not just on their own platforms, and not just on social media. You've probably heard of cookies, which are used to track our online behaviours, but you might not have heard of _'tracking pixels'_ - tiny images embedded in websites to perform a similar function. AHow about browser fingerprinting? Nope? There are a multitude of ways in which companies track our online activities (although that is a topic for another day). [Google]('https://duckduckgo.github.io/tracker-radar-wiki/entities/Google%20LLC.html'), for instance, has tracking embedded in more then 2/3 of the most popular websites, while [Meta]('https://duckduckgo.github.io/tracker-radar-wiki/entities/Facebook,%20Inc..html') sits at 21%. When that website you visit shows a popup that explains how they are sharing data with their 376 partners, they are really sharing (or selling) your data with 376 companies, many of whom are congregating your data to sell on again. Why? Because the bigger your footprint, the more valuable it is.

The good news is there are ways to effectively minimise your digital footprint. The even better news is that there are ways to minimise your digital footprint to a point where there is very little data on which to feed the algorithms to provide you with recommendations.

There is no one silver bullet. It isn't simply a case of use a VPN and all your internet traffic is anonymised, or install a browser extension and all trackers are blocked. Minimising your digital footprint effectively requires some technical knowledge of how the internet works. In the aforementioned example of sex education, we teach young people details about anatomy to help them understand how pregnancies and STDs occur. This knowledge helps them make smarter behavioural choices. In the same vain, we need to teach children the technical details of how our devices work. This knowledge will help protect them and make smarter choices when online.
