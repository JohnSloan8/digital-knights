So, we're going to be banning social media in order to protect our children. Or, at least it seems that we might be heading in that direction after Australia introduced the world's first ban for under 16s at the end of last year. Some of our EU neighbours have gotten the ball rolling on similar legislation themselves - the National Assembly in France has adopted a bill to ban under 15s, and the Spanish govenment have announced intentions to pursue an under 16 ban. Many other other countries, including the UK, Denmark, and ourselves, are at various stages of going down the route of some form of a ban.

### Why the bans?

So, why exactly are we banning social media for kids? The answer may seem obvious - because it's dangerous of course! But how dangerous is it? To answer that question, we can look to a number of sources. First let's listen to what those who are leading the charge in introducing these bans have to say.

**Anthony Albanese**, Prime Minister of Australia wrote that social media is:

<Quote>
  a weapon for bullies, a platform for peer pressure, a driver of anxiety, a vehicle for scammers
  and, worst of all, a tool for online predators
</Quote>

French President **Emmanuel Macron** announced:

<Quote>
  The brains of our children and teenagers are not for sale. Their emotions are not for sale,
  neither to American platforms nor Chinese algorithms.
</Quote>

And, Prime Minister **Pedro Sánchez** of Spain said social media is a place of:

<Quote>...addiction, abuse, pornography, manipulation [and] violence.</Quote>

This is heavy stuff. You would be forgiven for thinking that it might be better to rip up the entire internet infrastructure, encase in concrete, and shoot it out into space.

Behind the bluster of this political rhetoric, there lies a general idea that there are problems associated with social media use, and understanding the dangers and pitfalls requires a certain level of mental maturity. As Anthony Albanese noted,

<Quote>
  as we get older most of us get better at spotting the fakes and the risks and we build up the
  resilience to ignore the nastiness.
</Quote>

So, the dangers that exist can be more safely navigated by older teens. While this may be true for some of the dangers, this approach risks complacency which could cause other issues. I'll return to this later.

### What does the science say?

Is there any empirical evidence to back up the effectiveness of a ban on social media for children? Short answer, no. Since these large scale bans have only just come into force, the true impact will only become clear in years to come. There are some early reports of some users getting around the bans easily, and others feeling much better now they're off their phones. However, it is wise to take these individual stories with a pinch of salt. We won't know the results for a few years.

The good news is we do have an abundance of scientific research into the effects of technology and social media use on teenagers. However, problematically, the results and conclusion often differ drastically. Take the following three academic articles (which have all been quoted in various media outlets) as an example. First, [a large meta-analysis of published results]('https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000468.pdf') carried out by an international team of researchers in 2025 found screen use leads to 'socioemotional problems', which leads to further screen use, and worse socioemotional problems - a viscious downward cycle. Second, [a 2025 University of Manchester study]('https://academic.oup.com/jpubhealth/advance-article/doi/10.1093/pubmed/fdaf150/8371934?login=false') on data from over 25,000 adolescents concluded the opposite, that there is no significant impact of technology use on mental health. Finally, [A 2026 large scale (over 100,000 participants), longitudinal study (3 years) of Australian adolescents]('https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/article-abstract/2843720') at the University of South Australia found that moderate social media use was associated with the best well-being, better than both no use and excessive use. So, where does that leave us? Well, probably a bit confused.

Amidst the conflciting conclusions, one consistency does emerge across the majority of the scientific literature - the negative effects of _excessive_ use of internet, games, screens, social media etc. The term excessive use is used when these activities begin to interfere with normal daily functioning. And the driver behind the addictive nature of social media apps?.. the **'algorithms'**.

### Can't we just ban the algorithms?

If we are keen to stop excessive or problematic social media use, can't we just make the algorithms that feed us addictive content illegal instead of banning the whole thing? A lot of social media is good after all - staying in contact with distant friends and family, arranging events etc. It seems a shame to ban under 16s from these elements. A teenager on a recent podcast on the issue of bans pointed to her regular contact and continued friendship with a girl who had left her school to move to another part of the counry a few years prior. Something that was aided strongly by social media.

TD Paul Murphy recently proposed a ban on these algorithms in the Dail. He asked for the Taoiseach to support his party's bill:

<Quote>"to have toxic algorithms on social media platforms switched off."</Quote>

Unfortunately, this is not a feasible solution. In fact, from a technical standpoint, it is quite a naive proposition. We would effectively be banning math. The family of algorithms used to recommend content to users based on theirs and others' history of activity are called _'recommender algorithms'_. These are simply statistical models which perform probablistic calculations. They can also be very useful - recommendations for music, movies, and series on streaming platforms can be a good way to encounter new artists, directors, and actors. Same for online bookstores, clothes shops etc. Could we ban recommender algorithms for one platform but not another? It would also be very hard to determine what is and is not a 'toxic algorithm'. ChatGPT is a Large Language Model (LLM), but it can also act as a recommender system. Do we ban LLMs? Additionally, this type of regulation would be impossible to police. Private tech companies keep their proprietary code under wraps. Unless we force companies to make all code open source, this proposal is a non-starter.

### What can be done?

Education. I will admit I am very biased on this topic. As an educator, when I watch the news or read articles on the need for social media bans, I am always on the lookout for information on what the education for children is going be when the bans come into effect. It has been frustrating and disappointing to see a paucity of coverage and a lack of details on this matter to date. Australia, despite leading the way and having a ban already in place, has not published updates in relation to the aage restrictions. The eSafety Commisioner's site, which provides educational resources for schools, still shows a "(updates in relation to social media age restrictions coming soon)" notice.

Banning an activity for children prior to figuring out the details on how to prepare them for it is putting the cart before the horse. When we (the adults) restrict a potentially dangerous activity until a certain age, e.g. alcohol consumption, sexual relationships, we are obligated to provide an education so that children are informed and prepared when they do come of age. For alcohol, kids learn the details of what a 'unit' of alcohol is, how much is in each type of drink and the short and long term effects on the health of the body. With sex education, we teach the details of the menstrual cycle, pregnancy and STDs, alongside consent and relationships. These topics are taught for years before coming of age.

Children themselves have been requesting more, better, and earlier education for digital safety for some time, including here in Ireland. The Ombudsman for Children's Office (OCO) said [blanket bans for phones in schools was not the best solution]('https://www.rte.ie/news/ireland/2025/0910/1532761-phone-bans-ireland/'). A report into this issue, '[
One Size Does Not Fit All (2025)]("https://www.oco.ie/app/uploads/2025/09/OCO-Smartphone-Ban-Child-Friendly-Report.pdf")' listed five recommendations by a Youth Advisory Panel which includes the following:

- _"Invest more in resources for digital education"_
- _"Increase support, education and awareness for parents, teachers and other adults to better understand technology"_

CyberSafeKids - Ireland's online safety charity - published a [Trends and Usage Report](https://www.cybersafekids.ie/report2025/) in 2025 where they state "Starting digital media and literacy education at secondary level is simply too late".

### Once the school curriculums are updated, won't they be sufficient to prepare children for ?

An education will help in many ways. To focus on the issue at hand for this article - problematic social media use in children - an education on some of the technical details of how the internet works can massively reduce your digital footprint.

Remember the recommender algorithms mentioned previously. They rely on knowing information about you in order to effectively keep you engaged. The more they know, the better the recommendations will be (and by better I mean they will keep you scrolling for longer and clicking on more adverts). Companies need to know who you are, where you have been, what you have been looking at etc. in order to be able to effectively target content to you. The larger your digital footprint, the more data companies have to use in their models to accurately categorise you and predict your behaviour.

The currently available educational resources in Ireland for children do not teach about minimising your digital footprint. In some cases, the advice provided by some would only make it worse.

### What can be done?

Unfortunately, there is no silver bullet. It isn't simply a case of use a VPN and all your internet traffic is anonymised, or install a browser extension and all trackers are blocked. Minimising your digital footprint effectively requires some technical knowledge of how the internet works. In the aforementioned example of sex education, we teach young people details about anatomy to help them understand how pregnancies and STDs occur. This knowledge helps them make smarter behavioural choices. In the same vain, we need to teach children the technical details of how our devices work. THis knowledge will help protect them and make smarter choices when online.

### And on turning 16?

Those of us who have worked a long time in tech and cybersecurity often forget that what is everyday, common knowledge to us and our peers, often is unknown by a large protion of the general public.

Your child doesn't use Instagram, TikTok, Whatsapp etc until they are 16. So, when they do turn 16 and log on, they begin with a clean slate, right? If they are careful with what they view and post, don't provide personal information, then they will keep their digital footprint minimal. No! Not at all.
