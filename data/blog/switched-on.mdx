On 5th March, the governemnt's Online Safety Commissioner, Niamh Hodnett, announced the introduction of a new Digital Citizenship programme for 5th and 6th Class called [Switched On]('https://www.oidetechnologyineducation.ie/new-switched-on-programme-empowers-primary-pupils-to-become-safe-smart-and-responsible-digital-citizens/'). It aims to <i>"...empower primary pupils to become bafe, smart and responsible digital ditizens"</i>. This is a welcome addition to the primary school curriculum. In the previous article - [Social Media Bans for children - Not a Panacea]('/blog/bans') - I had highlighted the fact that Australia had introduced their ban on social media for under 16s before providing any details on the education the affected children would receive. I was critical of this approach, claiming it was putting the cart before the horse. It is good to see the Irish government setting out their education plans before any potential ban is brought in here.

In this article, I want to provide an independent, critical review of the Switched On programme. Before getting into the details, it is worth explaining what that means in plain terms. A critical review is not about being negative for the sake of it. It means looking carefully at something, asking what works, what doesn't, what might be missing, and what could be improved. The purpose is simple: make the final result better. So when I point out weaknesses, I am not attacking the people who created the programme, I am trying to help stress-test it. I am also trying to understand the gaps which can be addressed by the independent curriculum being developed here on Digital Knights.

The format of the review will follow a simple sturcture, one that should be familiar to all: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly. Unfortunatly, things do get quite ugly, but we'll leave that until later.

###

Now for the ugly. You would be forgiven if you are wondering how this can get any uglier. The last section was . Well, it does. However, I feel I need to preface this section with a story that will hopefully illustrate why I have chosen to highlight the criticisms that follow below. Here goes...

Last year, my wife and I put in a bid on a house we both really liked. We viewed it three times, once accompanied by my father-in-law, a man who is more knowledegable in this area than either of us. All three of us were confident the current bid was a good bit below what the house was worth, and if we could get it at anywhere near that price, it would be a steal. After a few days, and with no further movement from other bidders, we were starting to get excited about this property becoming our forever home. However, the excitement was accompanied with a side of anxiety (or maybe 'bidder's regret') - why is no-one else bidding? Are there problems that we didn't notice? So, we decided to hire a builder to give it the once over and let us know if there were any issues.

The builder spent 30 minutes in the house, investigating every nook and cranny. WE sat down together in a cafe afterwards where he listed numerous issues that indicated the two existing extensions were not constructed by professionals. These included the pitch of the conservatory roof being too shallow, incorrectly installed plumbing, and settlement of brickwork. He concluded that this was a DIY job by an amateur, something that is quite common for extensions completed in the 80s and 90s. If we bought the house, the extensions would all need to be completely reconstructed, and it would cost upwards of 300k.

We did not have 300k to spend on top of our bid, so we pulled out. But we learned a valuable lesson - get the experts in!

Ok, story over. How does this relate to 'Switched On'? Well, our builder was able to infer, from the quality of the construction work on the extensions, that the person/people who carried it out were not experts. There were numerous basic errors and oversights that a skilled builder would not make. You would not want to live in a house constructed by this builder - it would be costly and dangerous.

As a technology and cybersecurity expert, when I read the Switched On curriculum, there are parts of it that make me feel the same way our builder did when he viewed that house. My feeling is that I don't think this has been designed by anyone with a strong technical or cybersecurity background. Or, if it has, then it has been done poorly.

To be completely honest, I went into reading this curriculum with some doubts already in my head.
