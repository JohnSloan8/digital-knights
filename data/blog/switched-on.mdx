On 5th March, the governemnt's Online Safety Commissioner, Niamh Hodnett, announced the introduction of a new Digital Citizenship programme for 5th and 6th Class called [Switched On]('https://www.oidetechnologyineducation.ie/new-switched-on-programme-empowers-primary-pupils-to-become-safe-smart-and-responsible-digital-citizens/'). It aims to <i>"...empower primary pupils to become safe, smart and responsible digital ditizens"</i>. Created by [Webwise](https://webwise.ie) and supported by Coimisiún na Meán, Switched On provides interactive educational content on a range of relevant topics. These include <i>"recognising misinformation and disinformation, staying secure online, managing screen time, and protecting personal information and privacy"</i>.

This is a welcome, and long overdue addition to the primary school curriculum. In my previous article - [Social Media Bans for children - Not a Panacea]('/blog/bans') - I highlighted the fact that Australia had introduced their ban on social media for under 16s before providing any details on the education the affected children would receive. I was critical of this approach, claiming it was putting the cart before the horse. It is good to see the Irish government setting out their education plans before any potential ban is brought in here.

Unfortunately, after reviewing the content of the curriculum, I don't have confidence that it adequately prepares children to become digital citizens. While some of the topics are very well covered, the content on 'staying secure' and 'protecting personal information and privacy' is relatively shallow. It provides very little effective information on these topics, and even contains information that could be misleading.

In this article, I want to provide parents with an independent, critical review of the Switched On programme. Before getting into the details, it is worth explaining what that means in plain terms. A critical review is not about being negative for the sake of it. It means looking carefully at something, asking what works, what doesn't, what might be missing, and what could be improved. So when I point out weaknesses, I am also trying to understand the gaps which can be addressed by the independent curriculum being developed here on Digital Knights.

The format of the review is as follows: A summary of the curriculum followed by three sections outlining The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly.

### The Bad

Generic Advice that doesn't work, e.g. "Choosing strong, unique, long passwords and not sharing your passwords with anyone.". We've all heard this advice, but the vast majority of us reuse passwords (or variations) across numerous sites (see [report]('https://www.forbes.com/advisor/business/software/american-password-habits/')). We need to provide practical instruction on the use of password managers, passkeys and how to check if your account has been compromised.

Informing students that they control settings, e.g. "Remember you can restrict access to
these features on your device to allow only when using the app, not
at all or ask every time you want to use instead of allowing access all
the time." This isn't strictly true. If you use an Android phone, then Google has root access to everything, which means they can accesss and share data without your say so. They were fined $314.6million by a court in California for these types of unauthorised data transfers.

Needs to be an extension to 'sharing'. Many people think that if they share a photo with a friend, then the photo exists only on their and their friend's devices. This is not the case. The photo exists on the cloud server of the platform you are using. If you send a photo by Whatsapp, that photo is now (and forever) on the Meta servers. Your friend receives a link to this photo on the server where they download it. Even if you delete the photo on your phone and your friends phone, it is not necessarily deleted on the Meta servers. Extend to backing up in the cloud.

There is an absence of information on how to effectively avoid targetted ads and content.
Limited information on cookies or tracking mechanisms. No advice to use privacy focussed browsers.

Lack of putting into practice! We are giving kids advice on what to do, potentially after they have received a phone, or months/years beforehand. We need to be siting down with kids and gradually bringing them into the digital world step-by-step.

Hacking no clear examples. Saying "If a game or app gets
hacked, your information (like your username and
password) could be stolen." it's very hypothetical, but this happens regularly.

again, the telling, not showing - "When visiting websites decline the
opportunity for cookies or clear browser
history/cached information regularly." This can be particularly difficult to do as certain companies make the process difficult.

"Use of secure connections (e.g. secure
Wi-Fi connections, avoiding suspicious
websites, HTTPS/Padlock)."
Use a secure wifi connection? How is this determined? WPA3 encryption? How frequently the admin password is changed?

"Look for “https://” at the start of a website address to ensure your
connection is safe." This advice needs to go. It gives the false idea that any https site with a padlock can be trusted. it only means the connection is encrypted in transit. It does not prove the site is trustworthy. Attack/phishing sites can get valid SSL/TLS certificates easily. In addition, we need to show childrent he difference between an encrypted message and an unencrypted one.

Better advice:

Check for https:// as a baseline, not a guarantee.
Verify the exact domain name (spelling, extra words, odd subdomains).
Be cautious with links from emails/messages.
Look for other trust signals (reputation, contact info, consistent branding, no urgent scam language).
Use browser warnings and security tools, but still apply judgment

Nothing on VPNs, Privacy focussed browsers.

The biggest glaring ommission is in the lack of any content related to tech companies overstepping boundaries and the ways in which they do. ### The Ugly

Now for the ugly. You would be forgiven if you are wondering how this can get any uglier. The last section was . Well, it does. However, I feel I need to preface this section with a story that will hopefully illustrate why I have chosen to highlight the criticisms that follow below. Here goes...

Last year, my wife and I put in a bid on a house we both really liked. We viewed it three times, once accompanied by my father-in-law, a man who is more knowledegable in this area than either of us. All three of us were confident the current bid was a good bit below what the house was worth, and if we could get it at anywhere near that price, it would be a steal. After a few days, and with no further movement from other bidders, we were starting to get excited about this property becoming our forever home. However, the excitement was accompanied with a side of anxiety (or maybe 'bidder's regret') - why is no-one else bidding? Are there problems that we didn't notice? So, we decided to hire a builder to give it the once over and let us know if there were any issues.

The builder spent 30 minutes in the house, investigating every nook and cranny. He sat down with us afterwards and listed numerous issues that indicated the two extensions on the house were not constructed by professionals. These included the pitch of the conservatory roof being too shallow, incorrectly installed plumbing, and settlement of brickwork. He concluded that this was a DIY job by an amateur. Apparently something that is quite common for extensions completed in the 80s and 90s. If we bought the house, the extensions would all need to be completely rebuilt, and it would cost upwards of 300k.

We did not have 300k to spend on top of our bid, so we pulled out. But we learned a valuable lesson - get the experts in! That house was eventually bought by another party, who have since pulled down both extensions, rebuilt and put on a complete retrofit.

Ok, story over. How does this relate to 'Switched On'? Well, our builder was able to infer, from the quality of the construction work on the extensions, that the person/people who carried it out were not experts. There were numerous basic errors and oversights that skilled construction workers would not make. You would not want to live in a house constructed by the builder of these extensions- it would be costly and dangerous.

As a technology and cybersecurity expert, when I read the Switched On curriculum, there are parts of it that make me feel the same way our builder did when he viewed that house. My feeling is that I don't think this has been designed by a aprty with a strong technical or cybersecurity background. Or, if it has, then it has been done poorly.

To be completely honest, I went into reading this curriculum with some doubts already in my head. I have frequented the [webwise.ie](https://webwise.ie) site many times over the last few months doing research for this project. Much like the builder in our house, I noticed numerous issues that bring into question the technical knowledge of the developer(s). Small things that the average, non-technical user would probably miss or think trivial, but stand out to an experienced head, e.g. misaligned layouts, button icons that don't click correctly, images that load visibly.

I passed the Webwise page for Switched On through Google's [PageSpeed]() tool and it failed. This tool is widely used by developers to test websites for common issues which affect performance. It provides a score out of 100 for performance, with a score of over 90 recommended. As you can see below, Webwise scores a lowly 59.
